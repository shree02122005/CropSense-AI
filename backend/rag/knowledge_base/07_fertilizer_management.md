# Fertilizer and nutrient management

## Start with a soil test
The correct fertilizer dose for a field depends on its available nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, organic carbon, pH, EC and micronutrients, which only a soil test can measure. Maps such as SoilGrids do not provide available N, P or K. In India, farmers can get a Soil Health Card from the state agriculture department, which reports 12 parameters (N, P, K, S, Zn, Fe, Cu, Mn, B, pH, EC, organic carbon) and gives crop-wise fertilizer recommendations. Krishi Vigyan Kendras (KVKs) and agricultural university soil labs also test soil.

## Soil Health Card nutrient classes (general)
Available nitrogen (alkaline permanganate method): below 280 kg/ha low, 280 to 560 medium, above 560 high. Available phosphorus (Olsen): below 10 kg P/ha low, 10 to 25 medium, above 25 high (labs may report P2O5, roughly 23 and 57 kg P2O5/ha as the class limits). Available potassium (ammonium acetate): below 108 kg K/ha low, 108 to 280 medium, above 280 high (about 130 and 337 kg K2O/ha). Organic carbon: below 0.5% low, 0.5 to 0.75% medium, above 0.75% high.

## Blanket recommendations versus soil-test-based doses
State agricultural universities publish recommended doses (RD) for each crop and region, usually written as N:P2O5:K2O in kg per hectare. When a soil test is available, the dose is adjusted: increased for low-testing soils and reduced for high-testing soils, or calculated with STCR equations for a target yield. Without a soil test, follow the local package-of-practice dose rather than a generic number.

## Typical general ranges (for orientation only)
These are common ranges in Indian packages of practice and differ by state, variety, irrigation and season. They are not a recommendation for a specific field.
- Irrigated rice: about 100 to 150 N, 40 to 60 P2O5, 40 to 60 K2O kg/ha.
- Irrigated wheat: about 100 to 150 N, 50 to 60 P2O5, 30 to 40 K2O kg/ha.
- Maize: about 100 to 150 N, 50 to 75 P2O5, 40 to 50 K2O kg/ha.
- Pulses (chickpea, pigeonpea, green gram): a starter of about 20 to 25 N with 40 to 60 P2O5 kg/ha, plus Rhizobium seed treatment; potassium where soil is low.
- Groundnut: about 20 to 25 N, 40 to 50 P2O5, and potassium as per soil test, with gypsum at flowering for calcium.
Always confirm the dose with the local agriculture officer, KVK or Soil Health Card.

## 4R nutrient stewardship
Right source (fertilizer type suited to the crop and soil), right rate (based on soil test and target yield), right time (split nitrogen into 2 to 3 doses matched to crop growth), right place (place phosphorus near the seed; incorporate urea or apply before irrigation to reduce losses).

## Organic and biological sources
Farmyard manure or compost (commonly 5 to 10 tonnes per hectare where available), vermicompost, green manures (sunhemp, dhaincha) and crop residues add organic carbon and nutrients and improve structure. Biofertilizers include Rhizobium for pulses, Azotobacter and Azospirillum for cereals, and phosphate-solubilising bacteria. Integrated nutrient management combines organic sources with chemical fertilizers.

## Signs of nutrient deficiency (confirm with a test)
Nitrogen: older leaves turn pale yellow first. Phosphorus: stunted growth, purplish older leaves. Potassium: scorching of older leaf edges. Zinc: in rice, brown rusty spots ("khaira"); in maize, white bands on young leaves. Iron: yellowing between veins of young leaves on alkaline soils. Many deficiency symptoms look alike, so a soil or plant test is needed before applying micronutrients.

## Amendments for problem soils
Acid soils: agricultural lime or dolomite based on a lime-requirement test. Sodic soils (pH above about 8.5, high exchangeable sodium): gypsum based on a gypsum-requirement test, plus organic matter and drainage. Saline soils: leaching with good-quality water and improved drainage.

## Safety
Do not apply fertilizer or pesticide doses that are not supported by a soil test, the product label or local agricultural recommendations. Over-use wastes money, pollutes water and can harm the crop.
